FairProp:

Fair Node Representation Learning via Differentiable Propagation Layers

Emmanouil (Manos) Kariotakis, Aritra Konar
Department of Electrical Engineering (ESAT-STADIUS), KU Leuven, Belgium
arXiv preprint, 2026 · under review
Paper Interactive demo BibTeX

TL;DR

Graph neural networks pass information between neighbours, and in real networks similar people connect with each other, so predictions can end up tracking race, gender or age. FairProp builds fairness into that propagation step: after every propagation layer, a closed-form projection equalizes the group means of the node embeddings. The same layer gives fair node classification, link prediction and node regression, converges provably, and costs less than one propagation step.

Abstract

Graph neural networks (GNNs) are the standard for node representation learning and are increasingly deployed in high-stakes domains. However, the message-passing backbone of GNNs can amplify topological bias, raising concerns about the fairness of their predictions. We study group fairness at the level of downstream predictions, spanning node classification, link prediction, and node regression, and derive bounds on the demographic parity gap for any number of sensitive groups. For node classification, our bound is provably no looser than the closest prior bound; for link prediction, we are the first to bound the parity gap of the deployed, sigmoid-activated prediction rather than a pre-activation proxy; and for node regression, we establish the first such bound. Common to all three tasks, our analysis reveals two distinct sources of bias: the separation between group means and the within-group covariance of the final representations. Guided by this analysis, we embed fairness directly into the propagation mechanism by augmenting the convex feature-smoothing problem solved by APPNP with a convex constraint on the group-mean gap and a within-group covariance regularizer. Unfolding projected gradient descent on this problem yields FairProp, whose layers consist of a propagation step followed by a closed-form projection, and which provably converges linearly to the unique fair optimum. Across three tasks, we empirically demonstrate that FairProp, even with exact group-mean equalization, provides a strong inductive bias that attains excellent fairness-utility trade-offs relative to prevailing baselines on benchmark datasets.

Why FairProp?

Parity Bounds for Three Tasks

We bound the demographic parity gap of the deployed prediction for node classification, link prediction and node regression, for any number of groups. The bounds reveal two sources of bias: the gap between group means and the spread within each group.

Fairness During Propagation

Each FairProp layer is an APPNP propagation step followed by a closed-form projection that equalizes the group means. The layers converge linearly to the unique fair optimum, and the projection costs \(O(nd)\).

What It Means in Practice

Three real decisions from the benchmarks, sentencing risk, citation suggestions and credit scoring, explained without equations, with the measured results before and after.

Strong Empirical Results

Against 18 fairness-aware baselines, FairProp gives the lowest parity gap on all four classification benchmarks, link-prediction gaps of at most 0.003, and exactly zero mean gap in regression.

What Goes Wrong, and What FairProp Changes

Many AI systems learn from networks: people linked to similar people, papers linked to the papers they cite. In such networks, like connects with like. A standard graph model shares information between neighbours, so a person's predicted outcome starts to depend on which group their neighbours belong to. FairProp adds one step each time neighbours share information: it moves each group's average back to the same place. Two Sources of Bias, Three Tasks

We bound the parity gap of each task's deployed prediction using two properties of the final embeddings: \(\delta\), the largest distance between group means, and \(\mathbf{C}_k\), the covariance within each group \(k\). The bounds hold for any number of sensitive groups.

Equal means are not the end of the story

Both groups share the same mean, so \(\delta = 0\). When one group is more spread out, a ring-shaped boundary still tells them apart, and a nonlinear readout can pick that up (Fig. 3).

FairProp handles the mean term exactly with the projection. For the variance term it adds an optional regularizer \(\lambda\,\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{H}^\top\mathbf{M}\mathbf{H})\) inside the propagation objective. In practice the projection alone already gives the headline numbers.

The FairProp Layer

Let \(\mathbf{H}^{(0)} = g_\theta(\mathbf{X})\) be the output of an MLP on the node features and \(\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{D}^{-1/2}(\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{I})\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\), with Laplacian \(\mathbf{L}_S = \mathbf{I} - \mathbf{S}\). APPNP's propagation layers are gradient steps on the strongly convex feature-smoothing problem

$$ \min_{\mathbf{H}} \; f(\mathbf{H}) := \tfrac{1}{2}\gamma\,\|\mathbf{H}-\mathbf{H}^{(0)}\|_F^2 + \tfrac{1}{2}(1-\gamma)\,\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{H}^\top \mathbf{L}_S \mathbf{H}). $$

FairProp adds a convex constraint that equalizes the group means and an optional within-group variance penalty \(\lambda\,\mathrm{tr}(\mathbf{H}^\top\mathbf{M}\mathbf{H})\), then unrolls projected gradient descent. Each layer is the familiar APPNP update followed by a closed-form projection \(\mathbf{P}\):

$$ \mathbf{H}^{(t+1)} = \mathbf{P}\big[(1-\gamma)\,\mathbf{S}\mathbf{H}^{(t)} + \gamma\,\mathbf{H}^{(0)} - 2\lambda\,\mathbf{M}\mathbf{H}^{(t)}\big], \qquad (\mathbf{P}\mathbf{H})_v = \mathbf{h}_v + \bar{\mathbf{h}} - \bar{\mathbf{h}}_{S_k} \;\; \forall v \in S_k. $$

Every node embedding is shifted by the difference between the global mean and its own group's mean, so all group means coincide after each layer.

Figure 1. The fairness lives entirely in the propagation block, so any readout downstream inherits it. With \(\lambda = 0\) the layer has no extra parameters.

Exact and Cheap

The projection is linear and symmetric, so the backward pass is the same operation. It costs \(O(nd)\), less than a single propagation step.

Provably Convergent

The layers converge linearly to the unique optimum of the constrained problem (Prop. 7). At APPNP's step size this holds for any \(\lambda < \lambda^* = \gamma\, n_{\min}/2\).

Project Every Layer, Not Once

A single final projection moves every node in a group by the same amount, so the minority absorbs most of the shift (Remark 16). Projecting at every step gives the smoothest representation with equal means, and the correction diffuses through the graph (Theorem 14).

A Tunable Tolerance

Replacing the exact constraint with a slab of width \(\delta\) gives a ReLU-like layer. Up to about \(\delta = 10^{-2}\) results barely move; on GermanCredit regression, relaxing recovers MAE from 0.481 to 0.448 (App. G.3).
